Featured analogs

For each target we keep windows that both of our scores put in the top 100 of the lesser-known pool, then take the best five at least 30 km apart. Each card says what matches, where the analogy breaks, and what a mission team could test there.

Where every place breaks. Earth has about six times the Moon's gravity, an atmosphere and a magnetic field, so dust, heat and radiation behave differently. No place on Earth has permanent shadow or the cold of a lunar polar crater. For the Artemis III targets the lunar rock type is not set, so rock is not compared. Each card adds only what is specific to that place.

Rock analogs under farmland and grass

The bare-surface mask keeps places where a rover could drive on exposed ground. Some places match the Moon on rock and terrain but are covered by fields or grass, so the mask hides them. They are useful for rock sampling and lab work, not for surface operations. The table lists the five best per target (at least 30 km apart) among windows that fail the mask, with their rank among all 22,030 windows. Some windows sit on rock the global map calls basic plutonic: Doğanşehir, Malatya, eastern Türkiye for the highland (rank 7 of all windows); northwestern Iran (Khoy ophiolite belt) and Karaman, central Türkiye for Nobile (ranks 1 and 2 of all windows). There the rock is most likely ophiolite, not anorthosite, so we mark them low confidence. Windows with less than 80% land are left out.

How we checked it

We never used the known sites to build the score. We used them only to test it. We test the score like an exam, and only with sites it never saw. We take places scientists already use as Moon analogs, and look-alikes: places that seem similar from orbit but have the wrong rock. We pair every analog window with every look-alike window (for the mare, 34 x 204 = 6,936 pairs) and count how often the analog gets the higher score. That share is the AUC: 1.0 means always, 0.5 means no better than a coin flip. The 95% interval comes from redrawing whole sites and whole look-alike regions 2,000 times.

Read the mare row with care. We picked the hard negatives because their rock differs from the target, so it is expected that the rock term separates them; the real finding is that terrain alone fails (0.43). The step from rock alone (0.82) to rock plus terrain (0.92) suggests that terrain adds to rock, but the two intervals overlap, so it is not proven.

Honest notes
  • Imbrium has one known site (Rio Grande Gorge), so its AUC of 0.90 has no interval. Terrain alone does well there (0.84), but one site is not much evidence.
  • The highland result (0.53, interval 0.29 to 0.83) is not a real test. The rock map (GLiM) labels both known highland sites with the wrong rock, so we mark highland candidates as low confidence.
  • We do not report how often a known site lands in the top 20. With 22,147 windows, a random ranking almost never does that either, so the number says little. AUC compares known sites with look-alikes directly.

Artemis III

The nine Artemis III candidate regions are near the lunar south pole. We do not know their rock type well, so we rank them by terrain only. To check our slopes, we compared our 10 km median slope with the region slope from LROC QuickMap. Ours is 0.87 to 1.45 times the QuickMap value, so the two agree in size.

Climate extremes among terrain matches

Among the 100 lesser-known windows that look most like each target in terrain, these are the coldest and the ones with the largest daily temperature swing (NASA POWER, 2001 to 2025). Even the coldest Earth window is far warmer than a lunar polar crater.

Water, ice and ISRU

Polar craters that never see sunlight hold water ice. These cold traps are the main reason for a polar base, and no place on Earth matches them. In our Artemis III windows, Diviner's thermal model finds that water ice would stay stable within 2.9 m of the surface under 7% to 60% of the ground. What Earth can offer is a dry, bare surface to bury ice in, or cold ground with real ice to drill. ISRU (in-situ resource utilization) means making water, oxygen or fuel from local material. The table groups Earth regions by what kind of water or ice test they suit.

Up to four places per group, by best score for this target. Every value in a row belongs to the best window of that group in that place. Aridity index: rain divided by evaporation demand; below 0.05 is hyper-arid. Full table: results/water_ice.csv.

Group rules, applied to each window on its own values. Dry-surface: aridity below 0.05 and more than 80% of the ground bare. Ice-bearing: passes the bare-surface mask, and snow or ice covers more than 5% of the window or the mean annual air temperature is below -2 °C (a common proxy for likely permafrost). Terrain: passes the mask but is neither of these. Cold windows that fail the mask stay "not a surface analog", with a note that they suit drilling trials only. Places marked well-studied are in Atacama, Iceland, Hawaii or Antarctica; places marked known site hold one of our known analog sites.

Method, limits and credits

Method
  1. Data. Moon elevation from LRO LOLA and SLDEM2015 (LOLA combined with Kaguya). Earth elevation from Copernicus GLO-30, land cover from ESA WorldCover, rock type from GLiM, dryness from the Global Aridity Index (version 3.1), climate from NASA POWER.
  2. Windows. We cut Earth into 10 km squares: 21 study regions, 759 half-degree cells picked automatically because they are dry and basaltic (20,572 windows), and 17 hard negative boxes.
  3. Features. For every window and every lunar target: median slope, steep-end slope (90th percentile), relief, and the main rock class by area. A DEM (digital elevation model) gives the slopes.
  4. Similarity. For each 10 km piece of Earth (a window) and each place on the Moon, we ask two questions. Is the ground shaped the same: similar slopes and height differences? Is it the same kind of rock? Rock counts for 42% of the score and ground shape for 58%. A score of 1 would be a perfect twin. The weights come from AHP (analytic hierarchy process, a way to turn expert pairwise judgments into weights): rock 0.42, steep-end slope 0.23, relief 0.23, median slope 0.12. Technically, terrain similarity is exp(-d), where d adds the weighted Earth to Moon gaps, each divided by its spread across Earth windows.
  5. Checks. AUC against hard negatives with bootstrap intervals, and a second, independent score from our first pipeline as a cross-check.
  6. Candidates. To find new places, we leave out the famous ones (Antarctica, Atacama, Iceland, Hawaii), the known sites and the look-alikes, and keep only bare or dry ground. A place counts as a robust candidate only if it is in the top 100 on two separate scores: ours, and the score from the first version of our pipeline, built with different settings. We keep the five best per Moon target, at least 30 km apart. That gives 65 robust candidates.

What we cover

Terrain (slope, relief), rock type, bare surface, dryness, Earth climate (temperature, daily swing, rain, wind), simple operations flags (landing and rover slope limits), and a water and ice reading.

What we leave out

Lava tubes and caves, biology, dust chemistry and grain size, magnetism, and the lunar environment itself (gravity, vacuum, radiation, permanent shadow).

Where it breaks

Earth is not the Moon

Gravity on Earth is about six times stronger. There is air, weather, liquid water and a magnetic field, so dust settles, rocks weather and radiation stays low. No Earth site has permanent shadow. In our Artemis III windows, Diviner shows the coldest spots averaging -233 to -177 °C over a year (40 to 96 K). An analog can only stand in for part of the Moon at a time.

Our method has limits

GLiM is a global rock map and misses small units. It shows the Black Point lava flow as only 8% of its box, and it does not show the Stillwater complex at all. We have only 15 known sites, and one of them covers Imbrium alone. Our 10 km windows are too coarse for small craters such as Meteor Crater, and POWER climate cells are about 50 km wide.

Why it matters

Mission teams pick analog sites mostly by habit: the same deserts and volcanoes come up again and again. This map turns that choice into a ranked, checkable list. A team planning rover tests on basaltic regolith can open the mare target, filter to lesser-known places, and see that the Arabian harrats match slope, relief and rock, with a link to the satellite view and the reasons the analogy fails. A team preparing for the south pole can see which Earth terrain is as rough as Malapert Massif, and that none of it is as cold.

Next steps: a finer rock map than GLiM, Diviner temperatures for every polar target, thermal infrared data (ASTER) to tell basalt from look-alikes from orbit, and the same method for Mars.
