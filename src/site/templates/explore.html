For each target we keep windows that both of our scores put in the top 100 of the lesser-known pool, then take the best five at least 30 km apart. Each card says what matches, where the analogy breaks, and what a mission team could test there.

How we checked it

We never used the known sites to build the score. We used them only to test it. For each target we compare windows from known analog sites with windows from hard negatives (look-alikes with the wrong rock). The interval comes from a bootstrap that resamples whole sites and whole negative regions 2,000 times.

Read the mare row with care. We picked the hard negatives because their rock differs from the target, so it is expected that the rock term separates them; the real finding is that terrain alone fails ($terrain_auc). The step from rock alone ($rock_auc) to rock plus terrain ($mare_auc) suggests that terrain adds to rock, but the two intervals overlap, so it is not proven.

Honest notes
  • Imbrium has one known site (Rio Grande Gorge), so its AUC of $imb_auc has no interval. Terrain alone does well there ($imb_terrain), but one site is not much evidence.
  • The highland result ($high_auc, interval $high_lo to $high_hi) is not a real test. The rock map (GLiM) labels both known highland sites with the wrong rock, so we mark highland candidates as low confidence.
  • We do not report how often a known site lands in the top 20. With $n_windows windows, a random ranking almost never does that either, so the number says little. AUC compares known sites with look-alikes directly.

Artemis III

The nine Artemis III candidate regions are near the lunar south pole. We do not know their rock type well, so we rank them by terrain only. To check our slopes, we compared our 10 km median slope with the region slope from LROC QuickMap. Ours is $qm_lo to $qm_hi times the QuickMap value, so the two agree in size.

Lunar temperatures from Diviner

Climate extremes among terrain matches

Among the 100 lesser-known windows that look most like each target in terrain, these are the coldest and the ones with the largest daily temperature swing (NASA POWER, 2001 to 2025). Even the coldest Earth window is far warmer than a lunar polar crater.

Water, ice and ISRU

Polar craters that never see sunlight hold water ice. These cold traps are the main reason for a polar base, and no place on Earth matches them. In our Artemis III windows, Diviner's thermal model finds that water ice would stay stable within 2.9 m of the surface under $div_ice_lo to $div_ice_hi of the ground. What Earth can offer is a dry, bare surface to bury ice in, or cold ground with real ice to drill. ISRU (in-situ resource utilization) means making water, oxygen or fuel from local material. The table groups Earth regions by what kind of water or ice test they suit.

Group rules: dry-surface when aridity is below 0.05 and more than 80% of the ground is bare; ice-bearing when snow covers more than 5% or the lowest hourly temperature in 2001 to 2025 is below -15 °C (a rough stand-in for permafrost). Regions marked well-studied are Atacama, Iceland, Hawaii and Antarctica.

Method, limits and credits

Method
  1. Data. Moon elevation from LRO LOLA and SLDEM2015 (LOLA combined with Kaguya). Earth elevation from Copernicus GLO-30, land cover from ESA WorldCover, rock type from GLiM, dryness from the Global Aridity Index (version 3.1), climate from NASA POWER.
  2. Windows. We cut Earth into 10 km squares: $n_named study regions, $n_auto_cells half-degree cells picked automatically because they are dry and basaltic ($n_auto_windows windows), and 17 hard negative boxes.
  3. Features. For every window and every lunar target: median slope, steep-end slope (90th percentile), relief, and the main rock class by area. A DEM (digital elevation model) gives the slopes.
  4. Similarity. One score per window and target, with weights from AHP (analytic hierarchy process, a way to turn expert pairwise judgments into weights): rock 0.42, steep-end slope 0.23, relief 0.23, median slope 0.12.
  5. Checks. AUC against hard negatives with bootstrap intervals, and a second, independent score from our first pipeline as a cross-check.
  6. Candidates. Bare or dry windows outside well-studied areas, known sites and negatives, kept only if both scores rank them in the top 100.

What we cover

Terrain (slope, relief), rock type, bare surface, dryness, Earth climate (temperature, daily swing, rain, wind), simple operations flags (landing and rover slope limits), and a water and ice reading.

What we leave out

Lava tubes and caves, biology, dust chemistry and grain size, magnetism, and the lunar environment itself (gravity, vacuum, radiation, permanent shadow).

Where it breaks

Earth is not the Moon

Gravity on Earth is about six times stronger. There is air, weather, liquid water and a magnetic field, so dust settles, rocks weather and radiation stays low. No Earth site has permanent shadow. In our Artemis III windows, Diviner shows the coldest spots averaging $div_cold_lo to $div_cold_hi kelvin over a year ($div_cold_lo_c to $div_cold_hi_c °C). An analog can only stand in for part of the Moon at a time.

Our method has limits

GLiM is a global rock map and misses small units. It shows the Black Point lava flow as only 8% of its box, and it does not show the Stillwater complex at all. We have only $n_sites known sites, and one of them covers Imbrium alone. Our 10 km windows are too coarse for small craters such as Meteor Crater, and POWER climate cells are about 50 km wide.

Why it matters

Mission teams pick analog sites mostly by habit: the same deserts and volcanoes come up again and again. This map turns that choice into a ranked, checkable list. A team planning rover tests on basaltic regolith can open the mare target, filter to lesser-known places, and see that the Arabian harrats match slope, relief and rock, with a link to the satellite view and the reasons the analogy fails. A team preparing for the south pole can see which Earth terrain is as rough as Malapert Massif, and that none of it is as cold.

Next steps: a finer rock map than GLiM, Diviner temperatures for every polar target, thermal infrared data (ASTER) to tell basalt from look-alikes from orbit, and the same method for Mars.
